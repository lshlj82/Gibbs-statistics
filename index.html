The grand canonical ensemble

A small system sits against a huge reservoir at fixed temperature \(T\) and chemical potential \(\mu\). It trades energy and particles freely, so even \(N\), the number of particles inside, keeps changing. Watch it happen below.

The Gibbs factor

As in the canonical case, the probability of a system state is proportional to the number of reservoir microstates compatible with it:

\[ \frac{P(s_2)}{P(s_1)} = \frac{\Omega_R(s_2)}{\Omega_R(s_1)} = e^{[S_R(s_2) - S_R(s_1)]/k_{\mathrm{B}}} \]

The difference now is that particles can cross the boundary, so the \(\mu\) term in the thermodynamic identity stays (the \(P\,dV\) term is still negligible):

\[ dS_R = \frac{1}{T}\left(dU_R + P\,dV_R - \mu\,dN_R\right) \]

With \(dU_R = -dE\) and \(dN_R = -dN\), each state gets a weight called the Gibbs factor, and normalizing gives the grand partition function, or Gibbs sum:

\( \displaystyle P(s) = \frac{1}{\mathcal{Z}}\, e^{-[E(s) - \mu N(s)]/k_{\mathrm{B}}T} \)
\[ \mathcal{Z} = \sum_s e^{-[E(s) - \mu N(s)]/k_{\mathrm{B}}T} \]

The sum runs over every state of the system, including every possible value of \(N\). With several particle species, \(\mu N\) becomes \(\mu_A N_A + \mu_B N_B + \cdots\), which is exactly what we need for blood.

Why carbon monoxide wins

A single heme site in hemoglobin is a tiny grand canonical system. It can be empty, hold one O2 (binding energy \(\varepsilon\)), or hold one CO (binding energy \(\varepsilon'\)). Near the lungs, \(\mu\) for oxygen is about \(-0.6\ \mathrm{eV}\). CO binds more tightly, so even a trace of it in the air steals sites.

How big are the fluctuations?

Problem 7.6. Differentiating the Gibbs sum with respect to \(\mu\) pulls down a factor of \(\beta N(s)\) each time, so with \(\beta = 1/k_{\mathrm{B}}T\):

\( \displaystyle \bar{N} = \frac{k_{\mathrm{B}}T}{\mathcal{Z}}\,\frac{\partial \mathcal{Z}}{\partial \mu}, \)\( \displaystyle \overline{N^2} = \frac{(k_{\mathrm{B}}T)^2}{\mathcal{Z}}\,\frac{\partial^2 \mathcal{Z}}{\partial \mu^2} \)

Subtracting \(\bar{N}^2\) and comparing with \(\partial\bar{N}/\partial\mu\) gives a compact result:

\( \displaystyle \sigma_N = \sqrt{\overline{N^2} - \bar{N}^2} = \sqrt{k_{\mathrm{B}}T \,\frac{\partial \bar{N}}{\partial \mu}} \)

For an ideal gas, \(\mu = -k_{\mathrm{B}}T \ln\!\left(V Z_{\text{int}} / N v_Q\right)\), so \(\partial\mu/\partial N = k_{\mathrm{B}}T/N\) and therefore \(\sigma_N = \sqrt{\bar{N}}\). The relative spread is \(\sigma_N/\bar{N} = 1/\sqrt{\bar{N}}\): large for a handful of particles, utterly invisible for a macroscopic sample.

It works beyond the ideal gas, too

The heme site above holds either 0 or 1 molecule. With only O2 around, \(\bar{N} = p\), and directly from the Gibbs sum the variance is \(\sigma_N^2 = p - p^2\). The grand free energy

Problem 7.7. The grand free energy \(\Phi \equiv U - TS - \mu N\) satisfies \(d\Phi = -P\,dV - S\,dT - N\,d\mu\), so \(N = -(\partial\Phi/\partial\mu)_{T,V}\). The candidate \(\tilde{\Phi} = -k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ln\mathcal{Z}\) obeys the same equation, since \((\partial\tilde{\Phi}/\partial\mu)_{T,V} = -\bar{N}\). At \(\mu = 0\) the Gibbs sum reduces to the ordinary partition function \(Z\), where \(-k_{\mathrm{B}}T\ln Z = F = \Phi\). Same differential equation, same starting point:

\( \displaystyle \Phi = -k_{\mathrm{B}}T \ln \mathcal{Z} \)
